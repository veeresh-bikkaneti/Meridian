## 1. What P6 is

A small, honest chemistry game — scoped to what US schools actually teach —
built around a real verb (combine-and-watch / classify / build-and-test),
not a quiz. The first real chemistry is grade 5 (observational). Grade 2
in the `7-10` group is only "take apart and rebuild" (2-PS1-3), with no
reactions. Chemistry as a quiz fails like a science quiz fails (council,
all seats). Its real verb is combine-and-watch: predict what happens when
things combine, then see it. Element Detective (solve cases using clues
from the periodic table) is the candidate format — only if the definition
supports it, and only in grades 9–12 and `18+`.

### 2.1 The reference standard: NGSS, PS1 — Matter and Its Interactions

The Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS, final version released
April 2013, developed by 26 lead state partners with Achieve, NSTA, AAAS
and the National Research Council) organize chemistry as disciplinary
core idea **PS1: Matter and Its Interactions**. The performance
expectations below are the scope, per band. Sources: California Dept. of
Education content-standards listing of the NGSS
(https://www2.cde.ca.gov/cacs/science) and the NGSS performance
expectations as published; accessed 2026-11-10.

| Band | NGSS performance expectations (chemistry) | What is actually taught |
|---|---|---|
| Grade 2 | 2-PS1-3 — make observations to construct an evidence-based account of how an object made of a small set of pieces can be disassembled and made into a new object. | Properties suited to purposes; taking things apart and rebuilding. No atoms, no reactions. |
| Grade 5 | 5-PS1-1 — develop a model to describe that matter is made of particles too small to be seen. 5-PS1-2 — measure and graph quantities to provide evidence that regardless of the type of change that occurs when heating, cooling, or mixing substances, the total weight of matter is conserved. 5-PS1-3 — make observations and measurements to identify materials based on their properties. 5-PS1-4 — conduct an investigation to determine whether the mixing of two or more substances results in new substances. | Matter is particles too small to see; conservation of weight through heating/cooling/mixing; identifying materials by properties; mixing can make new substances. This is the first real chemistry band — and it is observational, not symbolic. |
| Grades 6–8 | MS-PS1-1 — develop models to describe the atomic composition of simple molecules and extended structures. MS-PS1-2 — analyze and interpret data on the properties of substances before and after interaction to determine if a chemical reaction has occurred. MS-PS1-3 — gather and make sense of information to describe that synthetic materials come from natural resources. MS-PS1-4 — develop a model that predicts and describes changes in particle motion, temperature, and state of a pure substance when thermal energy is added or removed. MS-PS1-5 — develop and use a model to describe how the total number of atoms does not change in a chemical reaction (conservation of atoms; mass conserved). MS-PS1-6 — design a device that releases or absorbs thermal energy by chemical processes. | Atoms and molecules as models; chemical vs physical change (new substance or not); conservation of atoms; particle motion and state change with heat; designing with chemical energy. Still no balancing equations, no periodic-table prediction, no stoichiometry. |
| Grades 9–12 | HS-PS1-1 — use the periodic table as a model to predict relative properties of elements from outer-electron patterns. HS-PS1-2 — construct an explanation for a simple chemical reaction from outer-electron states, periodic-table trends, and chemical properties (e.g. sodium + chlorine, carbon + oxygen). HS-PS1-3 — plan and conduct an investigation to gather evidence to compare the structure of substances at the bulk scale. HS-PS1-4 — develop a model to illustrate that the release or absorption of energy from a chemical reaction system depends on changes in total bond energy. HS-PS1-5 — apply principles to explain effects of temperature/concentration on reaction rate. HS-PS1-6 — refine the design of a chemical system by specifying a change in conditions that would produce increased amounts of products. HS-PS1-7 — use mathematical representations to support the claim that atoms, and therefore mass, are conserved during a chemical reaction. HS-PS1-8 — develop models to illustrate the changes in the composition of the nucleus of the atom and the energy released during fission, fusion, and radioactive decay. | The periodic table as a predictive model; explaining reactions from electron structure; bond energy; reaction rates; equilibrium/product yield; conservation of mass mathematically; nuclear chemistry. This is the first band where chemistry is symbolic and predictive. |

**Confidence:** HIGH for the NGSS performance expectations themselves
(they are quoted from the published standards). MEDIUM for "what a given
child's school teaches" — see §2.2.

### 2.2 The adoption caveat (why this answer is evidence, not certification)

- NGSS was adopted outright by about **20 states plus the District of
  Columbia**; roughly two dozen more states use standards based on the
  same Framework for K-12 Science Education; **44 states** have
  implemented standards influenced by the Framework and/or NGSS (NSTA,
  "Ten Years of NGSS", 2023 —
  https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-marchapril-2023/ten-years-ngss-where-are-we-now — accessed 2026-11-10).
- **Texas and a few other states use their own frameworks** (e.g. TEKS).
  A child in Texas does not follow the NGSS sequence above.
- US curricula vary by state: science in elementary school is
  observational; middle school goes deeper, often with a physical-sciences
  focus in grade 8; the National Science Education Standards progression
  runs from a macroscopic world of observable phenomena (elementary) to
  the abstract world of molecules, atoms, subatomic particles, formulas
  and equations (high school) — TIMSS 2015 Encyclopedia, US chapter
  (http://timss2015.org/encyclopedia/countries/united-states/the-science-curriculum-in-primary-and-lower-secondary-grades/ — accessed
  2026-11-10).
- Upper-elementary physical science (grades 3–5 band) in state frameworks
  is: states of matter and their properties; how state changes by heating
  or cooling; classifying materials by physical properties (TIMSS 2015,
  same source). Middle school: changes of state, solutions, simple
  chemical reactions, properties of substances (NSDL Middle School Portal,
  "Chemistry: Making it Real" —
  https://nsdl.library.cornell.edu/websites/wiki/index.php/MSP_MiddleSchoolPortal/Chemistry-_Making_it_Real.html — accessed 2026-11-10).

**Consequence for the scope answer:** the NGSS band table in §2.1 is the
best national reference available, and it is the scope this brief
proposes — but the signing educator must confirm it against the
jurisdiction(s) Meridian actually serves, because a state on TEKS or
another framework will differ in sequence and emphasis.

### 2.3 The scope answer (the finding)

**US schools teach very little chemistry before high school — and what
they teach is observational, not symbolic.**

- **Grade 5 (bottom band):** particles-too-small-to-see, conservation of
  weight, identifying materials by properties, mixing can make new
  substances. No atoms named as structures, no formulas, no equations.
- **Grades 6–8 (middle band):** atomic/molecular models, chemical vs
  physical change, conservation of atoms, particle motion and state
  change, chemical energy in design. No periodic-table prediction, no
  balancing, no rates as mathematics.
- **Grades 9–12 (oldest band):** periodic-table prediction, electron-
  structure explanations of reactions, bond energy, rates, equilibrium,
  conservation of mass mathematically, nuclear chemistry. The only band
  where chemistry is a predictive, symbolic subject.

**Therefore the honest scope of a P6 game is small.** It is not a
general-chemistry game. It is, at most: classifying and combining
observable substances (grade 5), modelling particles and telling chemical
from physical change (grades 6–8), and — only in the oldest band —
predicting from the periodic table. If the educator's jurisdiction teaches
even less, the honest answer may be a very small game or a shelf — this
brief reports that possibility rather than inventing scope.

## 4. Core verb and mode

**Verb: combine-and-watch.** The child predicts what happens when things
combine, then sees it. Predict-then-see is two distinct moments:
prediction first, visible outcome second. A quiz-format surface is
dismissed by the council — do not mock one, do not build one.

**Candidate mode: Element Detective** — solve cases using clues from the
periodic table. Admitted only for the oldest band, because only there
does the periodic table predict anything (§2.1: HS-PS1-1/2). For grades
5–8 the mode is combine-and-watch on observable substances (mix, heat,
cool — what changes?), not a periodic-table detective.

**Fail state:** a prediction that is wrong produces a visibly wrong
outcome (nothing happens when the child predicted fizz; a new substance
forms when the child predicted a mere mixture) — not a score penalty
alone. A verb with no fail state is not a game.

**One mode, small.** The mockup must reflect the scoped size: a big game
mocked on a small scope is a scope lie. If the scope answer (§2.3) holds,
P6 is one mode with a handful of combinations, not a subject launch.

## 5. Judging rule

1. **Prediction judged against verified outcome rules** (§6): the child's
   prediction (what will happen? new substance / no new substance /
   which product?) is right iff it matches the checked outcome row for
   that combination.
2. **Near-miss:** right category, wrong detail (e.g. predicts a gas is
   released but names the wrong gas) is a near-miss — recorded as such,
   not passed. The near-miss taxonomy is a definition item for the
   educator to confirm.
3. **No partial credit in v1; no LLM near the judging data.** Judging is a
   lookup against checked data rows, never model output.

### Judging test plan

A test walks: right prediction; wrong prediction; near-miss; and a
combination whose outcome the data does not cover (must fail closed —
"we don't know" — never improvise an outcome). A judging rule no test
walks is UNVERIFIED.

## 11. Why first, bridge, use-it-elsewhere (standard §0)

**Why first (`7-10`, grade 5).** Hook: "The ice in the cooler is gone, and
the outside of the cooler is wet. Where did the ice go?" Counting the ice
cubes that remain does not answer it. Why, inside a minute: "Cooling and
heating move matter between solid, liquid, and gas. That is why the ice
became water." Illustrative scene, not a measured cooler. Objective: after
this, a child in `7-10` can predict whether heating, cooling, or mixing
makes a new substance or only a new state, shown by a tap before the
outcome plays. Standard: 5-PS1-2 and 5-PS1-4.

**Bridge.** States of matter build on heat as energy (map step Ph3) when
that unit exists; until it does, the bridge is measurement (M1): the child
already compares heavier and lighter, hotter and colder, as observations.
`subject-progression-map.md` step C1.

**Use it elsewhere.**
- Math: a mixture as parts (fractions), `7-10` concrete ("half the cup is
  water"), no concentration formula.
- Everyday: sugar in tea, wet clothes drying. The game does not give a
  recipe to try in a real kitchen.
- Environment: salt in the sea, and the water cycle (evaporation). Earth
  science only as the setting. No plants, animals, or ecosystems.
  MS-ESS2-4 is the environment link for the cycle (sun and gravity),
  already sourced in §10's sibling notes; living things are not added.

**`10-18` / `18+` why.** "The beads of water on the cold cup — did the cup
leak, or did the water come from the air?" Same loop. Grades 6–8 standard
MS-PS1-4 (particle motion and state). Grades 9–12 and `18+`: predict a
property from the periodic table (HS-PS1-1), Element Detective only.

**Loop.** Hook → predict → see the checked row → one sentence why → one
retry, then the review deck. Fail state is a visible wrong outcome, not
only a score (already §4).

**Mastery.** Confident = correct prediction on the first try (standard §4,
science bar).
